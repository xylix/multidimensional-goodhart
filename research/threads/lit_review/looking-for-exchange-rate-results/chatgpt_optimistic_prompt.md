## Ranked shortlist

**Closest prior statement: George P. Baker, “Incentive Contracts and Performance Measurement,” *Journal of Political Economy* 100(3), 1992, pp. 598–614.** This is contract theory / organizational economics. The model’s key objects are the principal’s noncontractible objective \(V(e,\varepsilon)\), the contractible performance measure \(P(e,\varepsilon)\), and a convex private cost \(C(e)\), with a linear incentive contract \(S+bP\). Baker’s strongest theorem-like statement is the one quoted above: **“\(P_e = V_e\) in all states of the world; this is a necessary and sufficient condition for a first-best contract.”** In your notation, \(w_j\) is closest to the score-weight vector embedded in \(P_e\); the missing/true objective is closest to \(V_e\); the separable quadratic action costs correspond to \(C(e)\); and the “measured set” is whatever enters \(P\). This contains an iff **alignment** result that is very close in spirit to your (D), but it does **not** quantify over all subsets, does **not** deliver your weighted-average harm formula (B), and does **not** study the sign of adding or removing channels as such. The target-deficit formulation \(\sum_j w_j a_j \ge d\) is also not explicit. citeturn18view4turn18view5turn41search1turn41search2

**Foundational multitask ancestor: Bengt Holmström and Paul Milgrom, “Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design,” *Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization* 7(special issue), 1991, pp. 24–52.** This is the canonical multitask distortion paper. It explicitly allows some activities to be measured and others not, and it derives how incentives on one measured activity are distorted by private substitution toward or away from unmeasured activities. Their clearest C-like statement is that it may be **“optimal to provide no quantity incentives when quality is poorly measured,”** and they even emphasize that a price clause can be worse than no contract because it distorts unmeasured quality. In your correspondence, \(w_j\) is the sensitivity of measured performance to task \(j\); \(h_j\) is the contribution of task \(j\) to the principal’s unmeasured objective or to neglected quality; \(\kappa_j\) lives in the private cost function; and the measured set is literally which tasks are observable / contractible. This contains the ancestry of **sign-ambiguous marginal measurement** and pins down the role of substitution across measured and unmeasured tasks, so it is a strong ancestor for (C). But it does **not** give your explicit formulas (A) or (B), and it does **not** state subset-invariant harm iff proportionality. citeturn40view0turn27view0turn27view2

**Best explicit measured-set statement I found: Jörg Budde, “Performance Measure Congruity and the Balanced Scorecard,” *ABACUS* 43(4), 2007, pp. 515–539.** Budde rewrites the Feltham–Xie multitask agency model under risk neutrality. The agent chooses a vector of actions \(a\in\mathbb{R}^n\); the principal’s true objective is a linear function with marginal-product vector \(d\); measured performance is \(P=Ya+\varepsilon\); and the optimal aggregate score is generated by weighting the columns of \(Y\). Budde’s congruity index is \( \phi(d,Y)= d'Y(Y'Y)^{-1}Y'd / d'd\), which is the squared cosine between the true-value vector and the best aggregate measured vector. The clean result for your question is this: if the scorecard is balanced and minimal, then **every measure matters**, and if some measures are not verifiable then first-best cannot be implemented by an explicit contract. More specifically, Budde states that congruity is **“strictly increasing in the number of verifiable measures,”** but the effect **“may even depend on which other measures are verifiable,”** and is **“not linear in the number of verifiable performance measures.”** This is the clearest non-AI formal statement I found that **dimension count is not the right primitive**. Still, it differs sharply from your generic (C): under his balance/minimality assumptions the direction is positive for congruity, not sign-ambiguous for hidden harm; and there is no analogue of your iff subset-invariance theorem. citeturn45view0turn46view0turn46view1turn46view2

**Strong cost-weight analogue: Wendelin Schnedler, “When Is It Foolish to Reward for A while Benefiting from B?,” *Journal of Labor Economics* 26(4), 2008, pp. 595–619.** The earlier 2003 IZA version is useful because it states the result very plainly. Schnedler studies multitask hidden action with a benefit vector \(\beta\), a signal vector \(b\), and a general cost matrix \(C\). His abstract states that the signal minimizing misallocation is unbiased **only under “restrictive assumptions on the cost function (equal marginal costs for different tasks),”** and, more strongly, that **“using an unbiased signal does not imply the lowest mis-allocation.”** The formal solution is that the signal maximizing surplus at a fixed signal-noise ratio is \(b^* = k(2r/\rho\, C + I)^{-1}\beta\); only when costs are isotropic does the optimal signal become proportional to \(\beta\). In your language, that is a very close analogue of the claim that the relevant object is not dimension count but the interaction between **value weights and cost weights**. It is especially close to your emphasis that proportionality/alignment results survive only under special cost structure. But it is a signal-design paper, not a least-cost target-closing problem, so it still does not contain your (B), (C), or (D) as stated. citeturn20view0turn36search0turn38view0turn38view1

**Best public-finance cousin: Raj Chetty, “Is the Taxable Income Elasticity Sufficient to Calculate Deadweight Loss? The Implications of Evasion and Avoidance,” *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* 1(2), 2009, pp. 31–52.** Chetty’s model separates behavioral responses into “real” earnings reductions and sheltering/avoidance responses with possibly different **social** resource costs than the private costs the agent equates to the tax wedge. The paper’s key result is a weighted-average sufficient-statistics formula: excess burden depends on **“a weighted average of the reported taxable income and total earned income elasticities,”** where the weight is the marginal **resource cost** of sheltering. This is not your exact channel model, but it is the cleanest public-finance statement I found of the principle behind (B) and (C): total social harm depends on the **composition of margins** and their social-cost weights, not on a single aggregate elasticity. In correspondence, the alternative behavioral margins are your channels; the tax-induced revenue shortfall is analogous to the deficit \(d\); the marginal resource cost of sheltering is analogous to \(h_j/w_j\); and the private optimization condition is the analogue of least-cost deficit closure. What it lacks is an explicit measured-set operator and any iff invariance over all subsets. citeturn34search1turn33view1turn33view2turn12view0

**Best environmental-economics cousins: Joseph Aldy, Matthew Kotchen, Mary Evans, Meredith Fowlie, Arik Levinson, and Karen Palmer, “Cobenefits and Regulatory Impact Analysis: Theory and Evidence from Federal Air Quality Regulations,” *Environmental and Energy Policy and the Economy* 2, 2021, pp. 117–156; and Carson Reeling, Richard Horan, and Cloé Garnache, “Breakdown of the Equi-Marginal Principle in Permit Markets Involving Multiple Pollutants and Exogenous Caps,” working paper.** These are useful because they are the cleanest non-contract papers I found that get closest to your environmental seed. Aldy et al. show that when a new policy is added in the presence of a preexisting policy, the change in co-benefits is sign-ambiguous: **“The answer turns out to depend on the stringency of the new policy, the technology parameters … and the cost functions.”** Reeling–Horan–Garnache show the first-best multi-pollutant condition as normalized equimarginality: the **“effective marginal cost of abatement,”** defined as marginal cost divided by marginal avoided damages, is equalized across sources; they also show that one-for-one intra-pollutant trading is not generally optimal in multi-pollutant second-best settings. ## How A and B map to named models

Your optimization problem itself is mathematically standard. In applied mathematics and OR it is an **equality-constrained quadratic program**: minimize a strictly convex quadratic subject to a single linear target. The KKT system gives the equimarginal rule directly, and the standard resource-allocation version is the textbook problem “minimize \(f_1(x_1)+\cdots+f_n(x_n)\) subject to \(x_1+\cdots+x_n=b\).” citeturn39view0

That means result **(A)** is not novel as mathematics; it is the diagonal-QP / equimarginal solution. Result **(B)** is also not conceptually exotic once A is imposed: evaluating a second linear functional on the optimizer always produces a weighted average of per-channel exchange rates, with weights induced by the quadratic metric. In economics, the most natural named homes are **equimarginal allocation**, **least-cost pollution abatement**, and **linear-quadratic multitask agency**. In finance, the closest structural twin is the Markowitz problem “minimize variance subject to a target return,” with a hidden factor exposure in place of your hidden harm; the math is the same constrained quadratic form, even though the interpretation is different. citeturn28search8turn26search0turn39view0

## What I did not find
