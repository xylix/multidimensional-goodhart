## Mathematical Deconstruction of the Proposed Theorem

To prove that the proposed results are direct corollaries of existing frameworks, the mathematical architecture of the colleague's claim must first be formalized and solved. The agent faces a constrained optimization problem where they must close a fixed shortfall in an additive score while minimizing a separable quadratic private cost.

The objective is to minimize the private cost of action:

$$\min_{a} \sum_{j \in S} \frac{a_j^2}{2 \kappa_j}$$

This minimization is subject to the fixed shortfall constraint:

$$\sum_{j \in S} w_j a_j = d$$

Here, $S$ represents the subset of available or measured channels, $d > 0$ represents the required deficit to close, $w_j > 0$ represents the measurement weights for each channel, and $\kappa_j > 0$ represents the inverse cost parameters characterizing the agent's efficiency in each channel.

Using a standard Lagrangian approach, the objective function incorporating the constraint multiplier $\lambda$ is formulated as follows:

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{j \in S} \frac{a_j^2}{2 \kappa_j} - \lambda \left( \sum_{j \in S} w_j a_j - d \right)$$

Taking the first-order conditions with respect to each action $a_j$ yields:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_j} = \frac{a_j}{\kappa_j} - \lambda w_j = 0 \implies a_j^* = \lambda \kappa_j w_j$$

Substituting this optimal action profile back into the shortfall constraint allows for the resolution of the Lagrangian multiplier:

$$\sum_{j \in S} w_j (\lambda \kappa_j w_j) = d \implies \lambda = \frac{d}{\sum_{j \in S} \kappa_j w_j^2}$$

Thus, the optimal action for each channel $j$ is strictly proportional to its cost efficiency and measurement weight, scaled by the total cost-weighted variance of the measured subset:

$$a_j^* = d \frac{\kappa_j w_j}{\sum_{i \in S} \kappa_i w_i^2}$$

The total hidden harm generated by this optimal profile is defined by the colleague as $H(S) = \sum_{j \in S} h_j a_j^*$. Substituting the optimal actions into this harm function yields the critical equation underlying both of the colleague's core results:

$$H(S) = d \frac{\sum_{j \in S} \kappa_j w_j h_j}{\sum_{i \in S} \kappa_i w_i^2}$$

Result (C) claims that total hidden harm depends only on the exchange rates $h_j/w_j$ and cost weights $\kappa_j$, not on the absolute count of channels, and that adding a channel can move harm up or down. This phenomenon is immediately evident from the functional form of $H(S)$, which is structurally a weighted average of the individual harm-to-weight ratios $\frac{h_j}{w_j}$. By defining a weighting term $\omega_j = \kappa_j w_j^2$, the total harm can be rewritten as:

$$H(S) = d \sum_{j \in S} \left( \frac{\omega_j}{\sum_{i \in S} \omega_i} \right) \frac{h_j}{w_j}$$

Because $H(S)$ is a convex combination of the ratios $\frac{h_j}{w_j}$, introducing a new channel $k$ to the subset $S$ will increase the total hidden harm if $\frac{h_k}{w_k} > \frac{H(S)}{d}$, and decrease the total harm if the new ratio is lower than the current weighted average. The total count of channels is mathematically irrelevant; only the relative distribution of the ratios dictates the outcome. This mathematical reality completely accounts for Result (C).

Result (D) claims that the hidden harm $H(S)$ is invariant across all subsets $S$ if and only if $h_j = c \cdot w_j$ for a common constant $c$, implying that the harm rate is perfectly proportional to the score weight across the whole pool. The proof of this condition is a trivial application of linear algebra. For the sufficiency condition, if $h_j = c w_j$ holds for all $j$, then the harm function becomes $H(S) = d \frac{\sum_{j \in S} \kappa_j w_j (c w_j)}{\sum_{i \in S} \kappa_i w_i^2} = c \cdot d$. This value is strictly independent of the choice of the subset $S$. For the necessity condition, if $H(S)$ is a constant $K$ for all possible subsets $S$, it must hold for any singleton subset $S = \{j\}$. For a singleton, the equation collapses to $H(\{j\}) = d \frac{\kappa_j w_j h_j}{\kappa_j w_j^2} = d \frac{h_j}{w_j}$. Since this must equal $K$ for all $j$, it necessitates that $\frac{h_j}{w_j} = \frac{K}{d} = c$. Thus, $h_j = c w_j$ is a strictly necessary condition for subset invariance.

The proposition advanced by the colleague is simply the projection of a vector onto a subspace defined by a linear constraint. If the unmeasured "harm" vector is collinear with the "measurement" vector, the projection ratio is invariant to the subspace, represented here by the measured subset. The claim to novelty relies entirely on the specific vocabulary used, such as "channels," "shortfall," and "hidden harm." Translating this vocabulary into the established nomenclature of various economic disciplines reveals a deep, thorough, and highly documented historical precedent.

## Contract Theory and Multi-Task Agency

The most direct and comprehensive mapping of this mathematical structure resides in the multi-task principal-agent literature. The foundational works of Holmström and Milgrom (1991), Feltham and Xie (1994), and Baker (2002) rigorously explore how agents substitute effort across multiple dimensions when evaluated by imperfect, incomplete, or distorted performance measures. This literature provides the exact mathematical framework utilized by the colleague, framing the "hidden harm" as the divergence between measured performance and true organizational value.

In the agency framework, an agent chooses a multidimensional effort vector $\mathbf{a}$ to maximize their personal payoff minus the private cost of effort. This cost is traditionally modeled as a strictly convex, separable quadratic function $C(\mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{a}^T C \mathbf{a}$, where $C$ is a diagonal matrix of cost parameters. The principal cares about the true value generated by the agent, defined as $V = \mathbf{f}^T \mathbf{a} + \epsilon$. However, $V$ is typically unobservable, non-contractible, or subject to extreme variance. Instead, the principal designs a contract based on a verifiable, albeit imperfect, performance measure $P = \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{a} + \phi$.

If the principal offers a linear incentive contract paying a piece-rate $b$ for the measured performance $P$, the risk-neutral agent maximizes $b \mathbf{g}^T \mathbf{a} - \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{a}^T C \mathbf{a}$. The first-order conditions yield the agent's optimal effort allocation as $\mathbf{a}^* = b C^{-1} \mathbf{g}$. To map this established agency model to the colleague's theorem, the agent's effort vector $\mathbf{a}$ corresponds precisely to the action profile across channels. The diagonal cost matrix $C$ contains entries $1/\kappa_j$, making the private cost $\sum \frac{a_j^2}{2 \kappa_j}$. The performance measure sensitivities $\mathbf{g}$ correspond to the score weights $w_j$. The principal's true value weights $\mathbf{f}$ correspond to the negative of the hidden harm rates, $-h_j$, as any effort allocated to a task with a low true value but a high measured score actively harms the principal relative to the optimal baseline. The agent's optimization under a linear piece-rate $b$ yields $a_j = b \kappa_j w_j$.

The divergence between the colleague's setup and the standard agency model lies purely in the nature of the constraint. The colleague dictates that the agent must hit a fixed shortfall $d$, meaning the agent minimizes cost subject to the rigid equality $\sum w_j a_j = d$. The standard agency literature evaluates an unconstrained agent responding to a continuously optimized piece rate $b$. However, mathematically, these are dual problems. The Lagrangian multiplier $\lambda$ in the colleague's model is exactly equivalent to the piece-rate $b$ required to induce a total measured performance of $d$. Setting $b = \lambda = \frac{d}{\sum \kappa_j w_j^2}$ yields identical effort allocations. The colleague has simply taken the dual of the classic multi-task agency problem and solved it for a fixed output target rather than an optimized surplus.

The agency literature establishes that the true value created by the agent for the principal under this incentive scheme is $V = b \mathbf{f}^T C^{-1} \mathbf{g}$. The measured performance is $P = b \mathbf{g}^T C^{-1} \mathbf{g}$. The ratio of true value to measured performance is exactly the term evaluated in the colleague's theorem:

$$\frac{V}{P} = \frac{\mathbf{f}^T C^{-1} \mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{g}^T C^{-1} \mathbf{g}} = \frac{\sum \kappa_j w_j f_j}{\sum \kappa_j w_j^2}$$

Substituting $f_j = -h_j$ into this equation yields the exact negative of the colleague's hidden harm ratio. The agency literature defines the "distortion" of a performance measure using the cosine of the angle between the true value vector and the performance measure vector in the cost-weighted space. A performance measure is defined as "perfectly congruent" with zero distortion ($\cos \theta = 1$) if and only if the performance measure sensitivities are perfectly proportional to the principal's true value sensitivities. In vector notation, this is $\mathbf{g} = c \mathbf{f}$, which corresponds exactly to the colleague's proportionality condition $w_j = c h_j$.

When perfect congruence holds, the ratio $V/P$ collapses to a constant $c$, completely independent of the cost matrix $C$ or the specific measurement vector $\mathbf{g}$. In the context of subset invariance as presented in Result (D), if the principal restricts the contract to a specific subset of tasks $S$—which is mathematically equivalent to zeroing out elements of the measurement vector $\mathbf{g}$—the ratio of actual value to measured performance remains rigidly invariant if and only if the remaining elements are perfectly proportional. The contract theory literature does not write out the formal proof of Result (D) as a standalone theorem because, within the agency paradigm, the subset-invariance of the projection ratio is merely a mathematical triviality underlying a much broader point: that perfect congruence aligns incentives optimally regardless of measurement noise, task exclusion, or the specific subset of metrics audited. Result (D) is an unstated, one-line corollary of the fundamental congruity requirement that has defined multi-task agency design for over three decades.

The logic of Result (C)—that adding a channel can move the harm up or down depending on the exchange rates—is equally foundational to the "effort substitution" and "gaming" literature. When a principal expands a performance measurement system to include a new metric, the agent reallocates effort away from previously measured tasks to optimize against the new composite score. If the new metric has a lower alignment with true value (a higher $h_j/w_j$ ratio) than the cost-weighted average of the existing metrics, the expansion of the measurement subset actively destroys value, despite increasing the absolute volume of data captured. This is the mathematical essence of multitasking dysfunction, formalizing why adding imperfect performance indicators frequently exacerbates gaming rather than resolving it.

Furthermore, Banker and Datar (1989) extensively analyzed the linear aggregation of signals for performance evaluation, proving that the optimal linear aggregate of multiple performance signals requires weights that are proportional to their sensitivity and precision. Their analysis demonstrates that the outcome is invariant to the specific grouping chosen only under strict proportionality conditions governing the joint density functions of the signals. While Banker and Datar focus on stochastic precision rather than deterministic hidden harm, the geometric constraints governing their linear aggregation theorems map directly to the subset-invariance requirements formulated by the colleague.

## Environmental Economics and Spatial Externalities

If the requirement for precedent demands an explicit treatment of "harm" and "subsets" rather than principal surplus and task measurement, the environmental economics literature on least-cost abatement and incomplete regulation provides an identical mathematical ancestry. This lineage, stemming from Montgomery (1972) and Baumol and Oates, evaluates the discrepancy between ambient pollution dispersion and aggregate mass emissions trading.

In this framework, a central environmental regulator seeks to reduce aggregate pollution to a specific target. There are multiple emission sources $j$, each capable of undertaking a volume of abatement $a_j$. The abatement costs incurred by these firms are strictly convex, typically modeled as separable quadratic functions $\frac{a_j^2}{2 \kappa_j}$ to reflect increasing marginal costs of pollution control. The regulator observes the mass of emissions abated, $\sum a_j$, or applies uniform administrative weights $w_j$ if the sources emit slightly different classifications of pollutants. The total measured abatement is legally constrained to an aggregate cap $d$. However, the true environmental damage—the hidden harm—depends on spatial transfer coefficients $h_{ij}$, representing the actual toxicological harm caused by source $j$ at a specific geographic receptor $i$. For a single critical receptor, the true hidden harm avoided by the regulatory program is $\sum h_j a_j$.

Firms operating under a uniform emissions tax or a perfectly tradable permit market will equate their private marginal cost of abatement to the prevailing permit price $\lambda$. This individual optimization yields an abatement profile of $a_j = \lambda \kappa_j w_j$. To ensure the market clears and meets the aggregate cap $d$, the permit price will equilibrate exactly at $\lambda = \frac{d}{\sum \kappa_j w_j^2}$, leading to the exact same abatement profile derived by the colleague for the agent.

The environmental economics literature has extensively studied what happens when only a subset of firms or regions can be monitored or regulated—a concept known as "incomplete regulation" or "spatial leakage". Result (C) is a well-known phenomenon in this field. Regulating a specific subset of firms minimizes the cost of achieving the measured target $d$ within that subset, but the actual environmental benefit (or the remaining hidden harm) depends heavily on the correlation between the firms' abatement costs $\kappa_j$ and their spatial damage coefficients $h_j$. Adding a firm to the regulated subset can theoretically increase total spatial damage if the newly regulated firm has a high $\kappa_j$ (cheap abatement) but a very low spatial harm coefficient $h_j$. In this scenario, the market mechanism actively displaces abatement effort from firms that cause more actual harm to the firm with cheap but environmentally irrelevant abatement, increasing the total hidden harm despite expanding the regulatory subset.

Result (D) maps directly to the theorem of optimal trading ratios. Montgomery (1972) and subsequent authors established that an emissions market achieves first-best ambient outcomes independent of the spatial distribution of the participants—or the subset of participating firms—if and only if the regulatory trading ratios (the weights $w_j$) are set exactly proportional to the marginal damage transfer coefficients $h_j$. If $w_j = c h_j$, the environmental outcome per unit of the measured cap is invariant to which subset of firms participates in the market or where the abatement physically occurs. If this strict proportionality does not hold, the ambient outcome is entirely dependent on the specific composition of the regulated subset. The divergence here is primarily semantic. Environmental economists frame this dynamic as the equivalence between an "ambient least-cost" strategy and an "emissions least-cost" strategy. The colleague frames it as "hidden harm" and "measured score." The underlying mathematics characterizing the linear constraint and the projection ratio are indistinguishable.

## Public Finance and Multiple Margins of Tax Avoidance

The third distinct lineage mapping to the colleague's claim is the public finance literature analyzing multiple margins of tax avoidance and the elasticity of taxable income. Pioneered by Slemrod (1990, 1995) and expanded through optimal taxation theory, this literature examines how individuals substitute effort and resources across measured and unmeasured channels to satisfy tax liabilities.

When a government levies a tax on reported income to close a revenue deficit, taxpayers respond not only by adjusting real labor effort but by shifting income into unmeasured, untaxed, or less heavily enforced channels. The taxpayer must achieve a specific tax liability or faces a target deficit $d$ to satisfy their budgetary constraints. The taxpayer utilizes various channels $j$ to minimize their exposure, such as offshore accounts, corporate retained earnings, or untaxed fringe benefits, representing the action $a_j$. The private cost of utilizing these channels—which includes lawyer fees, the risk of audit penalties, and the loss of liquidity—is modeled as a convex function $\frac{a_j^2}{2 \kappa_j}$. The "score" in this paradigm is the reported taxable income, weighted by the statutory tax rates $w_j$ applicable to different bases. The "hidden harm" is the actual deadweight loss to society or the loss of total systemic government revenue across all bases, represented by $h_j$.

Slemrod and others demonstrate that evaluating the efficiency of a tax system requires looking beyond the targeted tax base to the full spectrum of behavioral responses. If the government expands enforcement to measure a new subset of avoidance channels, the total deadweight loss or hidden harm does not necessarily decrease. As the colleague notes in Result (C), measuring an additional channel simply shifts the taxpayer's cost-minimization problem. If the newly regulated channel was relatively benign from a social welfare perspective, closing that loophole can push behavior into even more socially destructive and costly unmeasured channels, increasing total harm. This is a direct application of the Theory of the Second Best formulated by Lipsey and Lancaster, which proves that piecemeal solutions (such as adding a single channel to a regulatory subset) in the presence of multiple market distortions can worsen overall outcomes.

Result (D) in the public finance context states that the deadweight loss of raising a fixed amount of revenue is invariant to the subset of tax bases monitored if and only if the statutory tax rates (weights $w_j$) are set exactly proportional to the marginal social costs of the avoidance behaviors ($h_j$). This is a restatement of the Ramsey rule for optimal commodity taxation, adapted for evasion margins. If tax rates are not perfectly aligned with the marginal resource costs of evasion across all channels, any change in the subset of enforced channels will arbitrarily alter the total social harm, as the taxpayer seamlessly shifts their cost-minimization strategy across the non-proportional gradient.

## Index-Number Theory and Consistency in Aggregation

While the prior examples rely on constrained optimization and behavioral substitution, the algebraic properties of the colleague's formula have deep roots in aggregation theory. Specifically, the "consistency in aggregation" literature developed by Gorman (1968), Diewert (1978), and Vartia provides an exact mathematical precedent for the subaggregation invariance claimed in Result (D).

The colleague's core mathematical output evaluates the behavior of a weighted index representing the harm ratio:

$$I(S) = \frac{\sum_{j \in S} \kappa_j w_j h_j}{\sum_{j \in S} \kappa_j w_j^2}$$

In index-number theory, an index formula is defined as "consistent in aggregation" (or exhibiting subaggregation invariance) if calculating the index for the entire dataset yields the exact same result as calculating the index for arbitrary sub-aggregates or subsets and then aggregating those intermediate results. Diewert (1978) and Vartia demonstrated that for an index of this fractional form to be strictly invariant to the subset partition—meaning $I(S) = I(S')$ for any disjoint subsets $S$ and $S'$—a strict proportionality condition must hold across the components.

Specifically, the ratio of the numerator component to the denominator component must be constant across all $j$. Therefore, the algebra requires that $\frac{\kappa_j w_j h_j}{\kappa_j w_j^2} = c \implies h_j = c w_j$. If and only if this proportionality condition holds, the index behaves perfectly linearly and is invariant to subaggregation. The colleague has essentially re-derived the consistency requirement for a linearly weighted ratio index and wrapped it in the language of an agent minimizing quadratic costs. The conditions under which a ratio of sums equals a constant regardless of the elements summed is a property of fractions, not a novel economic theorem.

## Precise Locus of Divergence

The colleague's claim to novelty rests exclusively on a highly specific and slightly unorthodox configuration: evaluating the exact substitution ratio of an agent under a rigid linear equality constraint ($\sum w_j a_j = d$), rather than under a linear piece-rate maximizing an overall surplus.

In Baker (2002) and Feltham & Xie (1994), the principal chooses a continuous incentive weight $b$ to maximize the organizational value $V$ minus the compensation paid for performance $P$, net of risk premiums. Because these authors focus on finding the mathematically optimal piece-rate $b$ to induce efficient effort, they do not explicitly write down the algebraic equation for the exact harm ratio constrained to a fixed subset $S$ fulfilling a rigid quota. However, the proportionality condition $w_j = c h_j$ (perfect congruence) is identified by Baker as the unique, indispensable scenario in which the performance measure induces the mathematically optimal allocation of effort across all tasks, entirely regardless of measurement noise, task exclusion, or subset isolation. The exact algebraic reduction claimed by the colleague is nested within Baker's proof of congruence.

Similarly, in environmental economics, the focus is on setting emission taxes or trading ratios to equal marginal damages. The mathematical reality that a uniform emissions cap produces arbitrary and potentially catastrophic spatial damages unless the weights $w_j$ equal $c h_j$ is the foundational premise justifying the work of Montgomery (1972). The environmental literature assumes the regulator acknowledges this variance and either implements spatially differentiated trading ratios or accepts the gross inefficiency of a uniform standard. The colleague has simply taken the equation for the inefficient uniform standard, mathematically calculated the resulting error surface, and declared the calculation of that error a new theorem.
