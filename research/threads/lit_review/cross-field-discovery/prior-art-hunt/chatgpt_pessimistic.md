# Precedents for the six-piece measurement-gaming synthesis

## Bottom line

Most of the six pieces are not novel. Pieces about **congruity/distortion**, **sufficiency/lossless aggregation**, **corrective-policy Goodhart**, and **stakes or competition strengthening corruption pressure** already exist under established names in agency theory, accounting, public finance, and evaluation research. The strongest single source is **Feltham and Xie (1994)**: it already unifies an equimarginal multitask action condition, an exact proportionality condition for zero distortion, comparative results on when extra measures reduce risk or improve congruity, and a formal sufficient-aggregation or redundancy condition. On the public-finance side, **Chetty (2008/2009)** and **Chetty (2009 AEJ)** already give the sufficiency-vs.-externality logic in essentially the same structure. **Reynaert and Sallee (2016)** is the clean modern home for “corrective-policy Goodhart.” **Campbell (1979)** and **Braganza (2022)** already connect stronger stakes or competition to more corruption pressure. citeturn13view0turn14view0turn42view1turn18view1turn42view0turn24search2

The weak spot is **piece 3 as stated**. In the canonical optimal-contract literature, *adding a contractible performance measure to the feasible information set cannot hurt* if the principal is free to ignore it, because the feasible contract set expands and zero weight remains available. That is exactly the logic behind Holmström’s informativeness principle and Feltham–Xie’s redundancy results. So the claim “adding a measured channel can raise or lower harm” is **not** a standard theorem of the cited multitask accounting papers unless one adds a constraint such as mandatory positive use, fixed-weight composites, measurement costs, limited liability, or some other second-best restriction. On that point, the best verdict is **the mapping is wrong or, at best, a constrained-model corollary**. citeturn33search14turn32view0turn14view0

If what the colleague calls a “new multidimensional model” mainly packages these pieces together, the contribution is much smaller than advertised. The most charitable characterization is: **mostly repackaging of established results, with one claim likely overstated unless the model imposes extra constraints that standard agency models do not**. citeturn13view0turn14view0turn25view0turn18view1

## Closest precedent for each piece

**Piece 1: least-cost or equimarginal gaming load.** The closest named precedent is **Feltham and Xie (1994)** in the multitask LEN agency setup. They state that the agent’s relative effort across tasks equals the relative impact of those tasks on the performance measure, while first-best relative effort is proportional to the tasks’ contributions to the principal’s payoff. In the generalized quadratic-cost restatement by **Budde (2006)**, the agent’s incentive-compatibility condition is \(a = s y\) in the basic separable case and \(a(s,K,y)=\tfrac{s}{2}K^{-1}y\) with interactive costs, which is exactly the familiar “load is proportional to incentive weight times responsiveness or inverse marginal cost” structure. The older and more general root is simply the **equimarginal first-order condition** of multitask moral hazard, which Holmström’s Nobel lecture writes as \(\sum_i \alpha_i \partial k_i(e)/\partial e_j = \partial C(e)/\partial e_j\). So piece 1 is not novel; at most the novelty would lie in notation. The only divergence is interpretive: the classic sources write this as effort allocation or incentive compatibility, not in the modern “gaming load” idiom. citeturn13view0turn34view0turn40view2

**Piece 2: distortion or congruity, with zero distortion iff \(h_j=c w_j\).** The single best precedent is **Feltham and Xie (1994)**. They define zero noncongruity exactly by proportionality: a performance measure is perfectly congruent if and only if there exists a constant \(A\) such that the performance-measure coefficients are proportional to the principal’s payoff coefficients. That is the user’s condition almost verbatim. **Baker (1992)** gives the same zero-distortion set in slightly different form: after normalization, first-best requires the marginal product of effort on the performance measure to coincide with the marginal product of effort on value; more generally he says a measure is undistorted when it responds to actions “in exactly the same way” as value. The later **cosine-angle** formulation associated with Baker’s distortion metric and then explicitly synthesized by Budde makes the same alignment idea geometric, but the exact “weighted average of exchange rates” formula is a reformulation, not Baker’s or Feltham–Xie’s own statement. So: **exact precedent on the zero-distortion condition; reformulation on the specific average-ratio expression**. citeturn13view0turn8view1turn9view2turn0search1turn10search10

**Piece 3: set-monotonicity.** As stated, the closest literature does **not** support it in the unrestricted optimal-contract sense. In **Holmström (1979)** and the later literature summarized by Chaigneau, Edmans, and Gottlieb, an additional signal should be included if it is incrementally informative because it lets the principal deliver given incentives at lower cost; that is weakly beneficial, not potentially harmful. **Feltham and Xie (1994)** say extra measures have incremental value either by reducing risk or by improving congruity, and they also characterize when extra measures are redundant and should be ignored. They do **not** prove that simply adding an available measure can worsen outcomes. Nor do the accessible summaries of **Datar, Kulp, and Lambert (2001)** state such a theorem; they emphasize an optimal trade-off between congruity and risk in the weights applied to measures, not a nonmonotonic theorem in the number of available measures. Therefore the right classification is: **wrong if read as a theorem about adding feasible signals under optimal choice; only a constrained-model corollary if the added measure must carry positive weight or if there are other second-best restrictions**. citeturn33search14turn32view0turn15search0turn17search6

**Piece 4: scalar sufficiency.** Here there are several close precedents, depending on domain. In **Feltham and Xie (1994)**, Proposition 3 gives a no-loss condition for collapsing one signal set into another; the point is exactly when one collection of measures is sufficient and the rest are redundant. In accounting more directly, **Şabac and Yoo (2018)** and **Yoo (2019)** are the cleanest home for the literal question of one-dimensional sufficient aggregation in multitask settings: they say that aggregation without information loss requires at least as many aggregates as effective tasks, and in the normal two-task case there is **no one-dimensional statistically sufficient aggregate**. In public finance, **Chetty (2008/2009)** and **Chetty (2009 AEJ)** supply the exact welfare-analytic analogue: the taxable-income elasticity is sufficient only when the underlying wedge from sheltering does not create the relevant externality or differential social cost; otherwise welfare depends on a **weighted average** of elasticities, not the scalar ETI alone. **Banker and Datar (1989)** is important but weaker: it gives necessary and sufficient conditions for when *some linear aggregation is optimal* and characterizes optimal weights in terms of sensitivity and precision; that is not the same thing as a general lossless-scalarization theorem. So piece 4 is emphatically not novel. citeturn14view0turn35search8turn29view1turn42view1turn30view0

**Piece 5: second best or corrective-policy Goodhart.** The exact modern home is **Reynaert and Sallee (2016)**. They explicitly study firms gaming the measure that determines an externality-correcting policy and derive welfare effects of that gaming, including the possibility that consumers gain through lower prices even while the measure becomes less faithful. That is piece 5 under its own name. The nearby public-finance lineage is **Ito and Sallee (2015/2018)** on attribute-based regulation and **Jacobsen, Knittel, Sallee, and van Benthem (2016)** on sufficient statistics for imperfect externality-correcting policies; those are classic “imperfect proxy for the true externality” papers, but they do not center endogenous Goodhart degradation as directly as Reynaert–Sallee. The older umbrella is the **general theory of second best** and the Pigouvian tradition of constrained corrective taxation. **Frankel and Kartik (2019)** belongs only as a cousin: it has the same endogeneity-of-information logic, but in a signaling environment rather than corrective public policy. citeturn18view1turn19search3turn42view2turn38search0turn2search7

**Piece 6: selection pressure strength to harm magnitude.** The best verbal precedent is **Campbell (1979)**: the more a quantitative indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it is subject to corruption pressure and the more it distorts the process monitored. The best formal modern home is **Braganza (2022)**: corruption in proxy-based competition rises with optimization or competition pressure, and decreasing the intensity of competition reduces corruptive pressure. **Bénabou and Tirole (2016)** is a strong economics special case: more aggressive labor-market competition produces escalating performance pay and greater distortion away from hard-to-contract tasks such as risk management, cooperation, and long-term investment. An additional unlisted precursor in science is **Smaldino and McElreath (2016)**, where stronger selection for publication output produces the “natural selection of bad science.” So piece 6 is also not novel; what varies across sources is whether the mechanism is verbal, dynamic-evolutionary, or a specific contracting channel. citeturn42view0turn24search2turn24search4turn25view0turn37search3

## Verification of the proposed mappings

The proposed mapping for **piece 2 to Feltham–Xie and Baker** is mostly right. For **Feltham–Xie**, the proportionality condition is essentially verbatim: perfect congruity holds iff the measure coefficients are proportional to the principal’s payoff coefficients. For **Baker (1992)**, the equivalent condition is stated in marginal-response language: undistorted incentives require the performance measure to respond to actions in exactly the way value responds, after the paper’s normalization. The **Baker 2002 / cosine-angle** language is also the right geometric home for a scalar distortion metric. What is *not* verbatim in either paper is the colleague’s specific “weighted-average of exchange rates \(h_j/w_j\)” representation of hidden harm; that looks like a derived re-expression of the same alignment condition, not the source papers’ own metric. Verdict: **exact on the zero set; special-case reformulation on the numerical formula**. citeturn13view0turn8view1turn9view2turn0search1turn10search10

The proposed mapping for **piece 4 to Chetty, Banker–Datar, and Şabac–Yoo** needs sharper trimming. **Chetty** is the right welfare-economics analogue: a single elasticity is sufficient only under a condition, and once the condition fails one needs extra objects, often a weighted average rather than one scalar. **Şabac–Yoo** is the right accounting home if the question is literally one-dimensional lossless aggregation in a multitask agency: they state that one-dimensional sufficient aggregation generally fails in multitask environments. **Banker–Datar** is *not* the same thing. Their theorem is about when linear aggregation is optimal and how optimal weights depend on sensitivity and precision; it is not a general theorem that a scalar aggregate is lossless, and their own conclusion stresses agency-specific optimality rather than information-preserving sufficiency. Verdict: **Chetty yes by analogy; Şabac–Yoo yes directly; Banker–Datar only partial and weaker**. citeturn42view1turn29view1turn35search8turn30view0

The proposed mapping for **piece 3 to Datar–Kulp–Lambert and Lipsey–Lancaster** is the weakest of the lot. I did not find an explicit theorem in the cited sources stating “adding a measure can raise or lower harm.” The standard signal-design logic points the other way: extra signals are either useful or ignored. Feltham–Xie explicitly distinguish positive incremental value from redundancy; Holmström’s informativeness principle says incrementally informative signals lower contracting cost. **Datar–Kulp–Lambert** is about the weight trade-off between congruity and risk, not about a set-monotonicity law in signal count. **Lipsey–Lancaster** helps only at the level of philosophical caution: under second-best constraints, local improvements need not improve welfare. Verdict: **not stated in DKL; only implied under extra constraints; wrong if asserted as a canonical unrestricted theorem**. citeturn32view0turn33search14turn15search0turn38search0

The proposed mapping for **piece 5 to Reynaert–Sallee and Frankel–Kartik** is asymmetrical. **Reynaert–Sallee** is exactly right: their title already says “Corrective Policy and Goodhart’s Law,” and the abstract describes firms gaming the measure that determines policy. **Frankel–Kartik** is not the same object. It is a general signaling model where higher stakes make a signal less informative to observers. That is a very useful cousin and probably the cleanest abstract information-economics parallel, but it is not specially about corrective policy or externality taxes. Verdict: **Reynaert–Sallee yes; Frankel–Kartik only analogous mechanism**. citeturn18view0turn18view1turn2search7

The proposed mapping for **piece 6 to Bénabou–Tirole, Campbell, and Braganza** is also asymmetrical. **Campbell (1979)** is the right older verbal home. **Braganza (2022)** is the right formal home if the claim is about competition or selection pressure causing more corruption of the proxy and worse goal attainment. **Bénabou–Tirole (2016)** is a strong but narrower special case in labor contracting: stronger talent competition raises high-powered incentives and thereby worsens multitask distortions. Verdict: **Campbell and Braganza are the broad homes; Bénabou–Tirole is a special-case channel, not the general theorem**. citeturn42view0turn24search2turn24search4turn25view0

## Sources that already unify multiple pieces

The decisive unifier is **Feltham and Xie (1994)**. In one paper they give: a multitask incentive-allocation condition that is the direct precursor of piece 1; an exact proportionality condition for perfect congruity that covers piece 2; positive-value versus redundancy results for additional measures, which is the relevant half of piece 3; and a formal sufficient-aggregation or non-loss condition in Proposition 3, which is piece 4 in agency-theory form. That is already a substantial synthesis, and it predates almost all of the colleague’s proposed unification. citeturn13view0turn14view0turn32view0

There is also a second, cross-domain unifier in **Chetty’s sufficient-statistics program**. **Chetty (2008/2009)** and **Chetty (2009 AEJ)** jointly unify the ideas that a scalar policy response can be a sufficient statistic only under a condition and that once externalities or differential social costs enter, one needs additional wedges or weighted combinations. That already ties together the logic behind pieces 4 and 5 on the welfare side, even though the domain is taxation rather than personnel incentives. citeturn27search3turn42view1turn42view2

The broad surveys the user flagged do shrink novelty, but less decisively. **Prendergast (1999)** and **Gibbons (1998)** clearly present multitasking and performance-measure distortion as established problems in personnel economics and organizations. **Holmström’s Nobel lecture** explicitly re-connects informativeness, multitasking, low-powered incentives, hard-to-measure tasks, and the need to consider commissions for all tasks together. But none of these sources, in the material I could verify directly, tie in the full sufficiency-lossless-aggregation apparatus or the public-finance corrective-policy side as tightly as Feltham–Xie plus Chetty already do. citeturn1search4turn1search17turn40view2

**Ridgway (1956)** also matters more than many economists realize. The paper explicitly discusses **single criteria, multiple criteria, and composite criteria**—including criteria formed by weighting and adding separate quantities—and argues that performance measurement has dysfunctional consequences. That is much earlier than Goodhart and already contains the managerial intuition behind “more metrics are not automatically better” and “weighted composites can create their own distortions,” albeit without the later formal agency machinery. citeturn1search3turn39search11

## Older roots and chronology

For the specifically organizational-performance-measurement problem, **Ridgway (1956)** appears to be the earliest clean precursor I could verify directly. Later retrospectives on performance measurement in policy and administration treat Ridgway as the mid-1950s starting point for the “dysfunctional consequences” tradition, and his paper already discusses composite weighted measures long before Goodhart’s 1975 monetary-policy remark. citeturn1search3turn39search11turn39search3

For the **second-best corrective-policy** logic, the roots are substantially older than the current Goodhart vocabulary. The umbrella theorem is **Lipsey and Lancaster (1956)**. The even older tax-correction lineage is **Pigou** and the Ramsey tradition: ideal corrective taxes exist in principle, but actual policy is frequently constrained, so welfare depends on what proxy can be taxed or regulated rather than on the true harm directly. Modern public-finance papers like **Jacobsen et al. (2016)** are best read as descendants of that older tradition. citeturn38search0turn42view2

For the **stakes or pressure** idea in piece 6, the older root is **Campbell (1979)** rather than Goodhart. Campbell’s law is already explicit that using an indicator for decision-making intensifies corruption pressure and distorts the process being measured. Goodhart names a related phenomenon in monetary policy, but Campbell is the better direct antecedent for corruption-pressure language and for the “stronger stakes, stronger distortion” proposition. citeturn42view0turn19search5

For the **formal agency-theory** roots of pieces 1–4, the main lineage is **Holmström (1979)** for informativeness and **Holmström–Milgrom / Feltham–Xie / Banker–Datar / Datar–Kulp–Lambert** for multitasking, performance-measure design, aggregation, congruity, and risk. By the late 1990s, **Prendergast** and **Gibbons** are already presenting multitask distortion and performance-measure choice as standard material, not frontier novelty. citeturn33search5turn29view2turn15search0turn1search4turn1search17

## Negative findings and audit trail

Two negative results survived the search.

First, I did **not** find a canonical theorem in the cited accounting or personnel-economics papers that says, in unrestricted optimal-contract settings, **adding an available measured channel can itself worsen outcomes**. The searches I ran for “adding a performance measure hurts,” “adding a measure can reduce surplus,” and Datar–Kulp–Lambert specific variants mostly returned trade-off language about congruity versus precision, or later summaries saying firms use other measures to “balance” multitask incentives, not a theorem of harm-increasing signal addition. The directly verified theoretical sources instead point to weak improvement or redundancy: Feltham–Xie say extra measures either have incremental value or are ignored; Holmström-style informativeness says incrementally informative signals lower contracting costs. So the colleague’s piece 3, if intended as a theorem about unconstrained signal enlargement, appears unsupported. citeturn16search4turn17search6turn32view0turn33search14

Second, I did **not** find a crisp pre-1956 organizational source that states the measurement-gaming idea more directly than Ridgway. I searched on **March and Simon**, **Blau**, and related bureaucracy or organizational-behavior roots; the hits were mostly retrospective discussions, bibliographic pages, or broad books on bureaucracy and organizations rather than a clean early formulation of metric corruption or weighted-composite dysfunction. That does not prove no such source exists, but it does make the negative auditable: the direct web trail turned up **Ridgway as the first clear hit**, with pre-Ridgway roots showing only broader organizational-decision themes, not the specific proposition. citeturn39search0turn39search1turn39search7turn39search11
