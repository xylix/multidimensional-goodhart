## Piece-by-piece prior art

**Least-cost gaming and equimarginal load.** The strongest prior source is **Holmström and Milgrom 1991**. Their multitask model makes the core move you need: incentive pay does not just induce “more effort,” it **directs the allocation of attention across tasks**, and when tasks are substitutes the principal can incent one task either by rewarding it directly or by reducing incentives on competing tasks. They also give the equimarginal condition explicitly in the perfect-substitutes case: the agent must equate the marginal return to effort across tasks. That is the right ancestor for the colleague’s “least-cost closing of a score deficit,” but it is still a **special case**, not the colleague’s exact load formula \( \propto \kappa_j w_j \), because Holmström–Milgrom do not write the problem as minimum-cost proxy gaming against a fixed scalar score deficit. citeturn12view0turn12view1turn12view2

**Distortion and congruity.** The strongest prior source is **Feltham and Xie 1994**, sharpened by the later economics treatment in **Baker 2002**. In the accounting literature, Feltham–Xie are the canonical source for **performance-measure congruity** and distortions in effort allocation across tasks; later accounting work describes them as providing “a useful characterization” of how aggregate signals distort effort allocation in diverse-task settings. Datar–Kulp–Lambert 2001 then explicitly frame optimal weighting as a tradeoff between the **congruity** of the overall performance measure and incentive risk. This is the right prior literature for the proportionality idea behind “zero distortion iff \( h = c w \),” but the colleague’s weighted-average “exchange-rate” statement is better read as a **special-case formal sharpening** of that line rather than a duplicate of an already-standard theorem stated in those terms. citeturn21search10turn10view1turn7view2

**Set-monotonicity.** Here I did **not** find a clean exact-match theorem in the canonical economics/accounting/management literature I searched. The best prior source is **Şabac and Yoo 2018**, but only as an **adjacent** result. Their abstract makes two points that matter: statistically sufficient aggregation requires at least as many measures as effective tasks, while economically sufficient aggregation can sometimes be achieved with a **single** measure if evaluation is sufficiently customized; hence job complexity, number of measures, and customization are jointly determined. That directly rejects the crude intuition that “more measured channels” is automatically the governing variable. But it still does **not** deliver the colleague’s stronger theorem that **adding or removing a specific measured channel can move hidden harm in either direction**. On this piece, the colleague’s claim looks strongest. citeturn8view0turn8view1turn8view3

**Sufficiency of the scalar aggregate.** The strongest prior source is **Şabac and Yoo 2018**, with **Banker and Datar 1989** as the key precursor. Şabac–Yoo are an **exact match** for the high-level question: when can managerial performance information be aggregated without information loss, and what minimum dimensionality is required? Banker–Datar are the earlier prototype inside accounting theory: they model the construction of a single evaluation measure from multiple signals, discuss when linear aggregation is optimal, and state that in their subclass **no scalar aggregate is a sufficient statistic except under specific conditions**. This is very close to the colleague’s piece on lossless scalarization. citeturn8view0turn8view1turn7view3

**Second best and corrective policy.** The strongest prior source is **Reynaert and Sallee 2021**, with **Lipsey and Lancaster 1956** as the theorem ancestor. Reynaert–Sallee are an **exact match** for the policy-gaming side of the piece: firms can comply with an **externality-correcting policy by gaming the measure that determines policy**, and the welfare consequences are ambiguous because gaming both erodes information and can reduce costs that are passed through to buyers. Lipsey–Lancaster supply the general theorem that in a world with multiple distortions, moving one margin toward its first-best value need not raise welfare; their classic statement is that removing one constraint may raise, lower, or leave welfare unchanged. Put together, these are the obvious prior-art roots of your piece 5. citeturn13view2turn18search0turn18search1

**Selection pressure and harm magnitude.** The strongest prior source is **Holmström 2017**, with **Frankel and Kartik 2019** as a strong adjacent economics cousin. Holmström’s lecture says that when multitasking is misaligned and the agent can manipulate the measured task, the principal ends up paying for worthless performance and the resulting waste is **greater the higher the incentive intensity** \( \alpha \). Frankel–Kartik obtain a closely parallel comparative static in a different formal setting: as incentives to take higher actions increase because stakes are higher or signaling technology is more manipulable, equilibrium behavior reveals more about **gaming ability** and less about the agent’s natural action. I would classify the prior match here as a **special case**: the monotone “more pressure, more distortion” result is there, but not yet written in the colleague’s regulator/proxy-vector language. citeturn24view4turn13view1turn23search5

## What existing sources already unify

Several prior sources do unify **two or more** of the six pieces, so the colleague’s contribution is **not** that every pairwise connection is novel. The standard multitask line unifies a meaningful subset. Holmström and Milgrom 1991 tie together effort substitution across tasks, indirect incentives on competing tasks, manipulation of observables, and the idea that incentives direct attention among measured and unmeasured activities. Holmström’s 2017 lecture then re-presents that line as part of a broader theory of incentive systems, explicitly linking multitasking, low-powered incentives, and the fact that stronger incentives on misaligned measures create more waste. That means pieces **1, part of 5, and 6** were already partially unified within organizational economics. citeturn11view3turn12view0turn12view2turn24view3turn24view4

The accounting line also unifies a subset. Banker and Datar 1989 unify **aggregation** and the **value of additional signals** through sensitivity and precision; Feltham and Xie 1994 and Datar–Kulp–Lambert 2001 unify **congruity/distortion** with optimal weighting of multiple measures; Şabac and Yoo 2018 unify **statistical sufficiency**, **economic sufficiency**, and the nontrivial relation between **measure count** and **job complexity/customization**. So pieces **2 and 4**, and some of what motivates piece **3**, already sit together in accounting theory. citeturn7view3turn10view1turn8view0turn8view1

Public finance and welfare economics unify a different subset. Chetty 2009 is a genuine synthesis, but it is a synthesis of **welfare evaluation from sufficient statistics** in taxation, social insurance, and behavioral public finance, not a treatment of metric gaming or performance-measure aggregation. Reynaert–Sallee 2021 then give a clean formalization of **corrective-policy gaming** with ambiguous welfare effects. Lipsey–Lancaster 1956 are the deeper theorem ancestor for why a piecemeal “fix one distortion” move can backfire. That means piece **5** already has strong local foundations, but again in a separate lane from accounting congruity and aggregation. citeturn16search5turn17view1turn13view2turn18search0turn18search1

The closest **modern conceptual cousin** to the colleague’s overall story is probably **Braganza 2022**. Its abstract says competitive systems necessarily rely on imperfect proxy measures, that these proxies determine the selection of practices and guide decisions, and that proxy measures tend to become targets and undermine the original goal. That is undeniably close at the slogan level. But it is **not** a source that already derives the six component results from the economics/accounting/management literatures you listed, and it does not replace the colleague’s cross-field mapping from classical contract theory, accounting aggregation, second-best welfare, and corrective-policy gaming into one common formalism. citeturn32search0turn32search1turn32search3turn32academia18

So the decisive verdict is this: **yes, existing papers unify subsets; no, I did not find a single source that already unifies the whole package.** More specifically, I found no single source that simultaneously combines **multitask effort distortion**, **congruity**, **set-sensitive channel effects**, **scalar sufficiency**, **second-best welfare**, and **corrective-policy proxy gaming** inside one formal framework. That supports the claim that the colleague’s cross-field mapping is a genuine synthesis rather than a rediscovery of an already-unified literature. citeturn24view4turn10view1turn8view0turn13view2turn28view0turn28view1turn28view2turn29view0turn29view1turn29view2turn29view3

## Missing but should-be-cited literature and subfields

A complete citation set should add several items that are **not** on your initial candidate list.

First, the colleague should almost certainly cite **Holmström and Milgrom 1991** and **Baker 1992/2002**. Holmström–Milgrom are still the canonical multitask ancestor for effort substitution, measured-versus-unmeasured task problems, and the incentive-intensity logic. Baker 1992 is the clean early statement that contracts based on performance measures not equal to the principal’s objective generally fail to give first-best incentives, and Baker 2002 explicitly characterizes performance measures by **distortion and risk**. Those are too central to leave out. citeturn7view1turn7view2turn12view0turn12view2

Second, the colleague should cite the **informativeness and aggregation** branch more explicitly: **Holmström 1979**, **Holmström and Milgrom 1987**, and **Kim 1995**. Holmström’s informativeness principle says an additional signal matters iff it carries conditional information about the agent’s action; Holmström–Milgrom 1987 show that accounting aggregates can be sufficient for optimal compensation in certain environments; Kim 1995 compares information systems by efficiency in an agency model. This line is the natural prehistory for the sufficiency piece and helps show exactly where the accounting aggregation literature plugs into general contract theory. citeturn30view1turn31search3turn31search5turn31search9

Third, the **subjective and relational performance-measurement** line deserves inclusion, especially **Baker, Gibbons, and Murphy 1994**. Their paper is explicitly about combining imperfect objective measures with subjective measures to mitigate distortions, and Gibbons 1998 relies on that idea in his survey. If the colleague is claiming a unified theory of multidimensional gaming under scalarized proxies, this literature matters because it shows one classical response to proxy distortion: supplement or override the scalar metric rather than only reweighting it. citeturn26search15turn24view1

Fourth, the organizational-economics corner around **Milgrom and Roberts 1988** and **Dewatripont, Jewitt, and Tirole 2000** should be cited. Milgrom–Roberts analyze influence activities in organizations, which are a cousin of metric gaming through political rather than purely productive channels. Dewatripont–Jewitt–Tirole explicitly survey multitask agency insights on effort substitution, conflicts between tasks, implicit incentives, mission, and task clustering. That is a different but neighboring “home” for pieces 1 and 3. citeturn26search0turn26search8turn33search2turn33search3

Fifth, the empirical distortion-detection literature belongs in the set, especially **Courty and Marschke 2008**. Their abstract states the basic empirical claim cleanly: distorted performance measures elicit dysfunctional or gaming responses, and one can test for distortions by estimating the change in association between the performance measure and the true goal after the measure is introduced. That matters because it is one of the cleaner empirical bridges from theory to observable gaming. citeturn26search5turn26search9

Finally, if the colleague wants the management genealogy, **Kerr 1975** belongs beside Ridgway 1956. Kerr’s abstract is the classic “reward A while hoping for B” statement, and Holmström 2017 and Gibbons 1998 both explicitly treat it as a precursor to the economics of imperfect performance measurement. citeturn26search2turn24view1turn24view3
