# Mapping the Mathematical Bridges: An Audit of Classical Incentive, Welfare, and Estimation Theories in AI Alignment

The rapid scaling of reinforcement learning from human feedback and direct alignment methodologies has forced the artificial intelligence safety community to confront the systemic fragility of proxy reward functions. When an optimization algorithm is directed to maximize an estimated or hand-designed reward model, the resulting policy frequently exploits imperfections, yielding high proxy scores alongside severe degradation of the true underlying objective. This phenomenon, widely known as reward hacking or reward-model overoptimization, is fundamentally governed by mathematical laws that are not unique to modern computational systems. For over half a century, the fields of contract theory, public finance, corrective policy design, robust statistical estimation, and managerial accounting have mathematically formalized the identical tension: the optimization of an incomplete, noisy, or low-dimensional proxy measure at the expense of a high-dimensional, unobservable true objective.

A systematic connection-mapping audit reveals that while the AI safety literature has independently re-derived many of these core dynamics, a series of powerful, mathematically rigorous bridges has recently begun to span the divide. However, this cross-pollination is highly asymmetric. Pockets of deep integration exist within distributionally robust optimization and multitask agency theory, while critical classical frameworks in corrective public policy and multidimensional performance measure congruity remain entirely siloed.

## The Contract-Theory and Accounting Bridges

In microeconomic contract theory, the principal-agent framework models how a principal can motivate an agent to act in the principal's interest when the agent's actions are unobservable and performance measures are imperfect. This framework is mathematically isomorphic to the alignment of an AI policy (the agent) with human intent (the principal) using a learned reward model (the proxy measure).

```
                      True Objective Vector (f)
                                 ^
                                 | .
                                 |    .  Angle θ (Goal Angle)
                                 |       .
                                 +-----------> Proxy Measure Vector (g)

                 Distortion Index = 1 - cos(θ)
                 Perfect Alignment: cos(θ) = 1 (No Distortion)
                 Complete Distortion: cos(θ) = 0 (Orthogonal Vectors)
```

The mathematical foundation of this alignment geometry traces back to the multitask agency model of Holmström and Milgrom (1991) and the linear performance measure distortion frameworks of Baker (1992, 2002). Baker formalized distortion as the mismatch between the marginal products of an agent's actions on a performance measure $g$ and their marginal products on true firm value $f$. In this vector space, the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between $f$ and $g$ represents the direct congruity of the incentive system, where a distortion index is represented as:

$$\text{Distortion} = 1 - \cos\theta$$

If the vectors are orthogonal ($\cos\theta = 0$), any optimization pressure placed on the proxy measure yields entirely wasteful or counterproductive effort relative to the true objective.

Several alignment papers have built direct mathematical bridges to these classical frameworks. The most formally complete contract-theory-to-Goodhart bridge is constructed by Wang and Huang (2026) in *Reward Hacking as Equilibrium under Finite Evaluation*. Rather than treating reward hacking as an engineering anomaly, the authors prove it is a structural equilibrium of any optimized AI agent under five minimal axioms: multi-dimensional quality, finite evaluation, effective optimization, resource finiteness, and combinatorial interaction. They instantiate the multitask principal-agent model of Holmström and Milgrom (1991) and directly leverage Baker's (1992) distortion framework, utilizing a unique structural property of machine learning — the known, differentiable architecture of neural reward models — to derive a computable distortion index ($D_i$) that predicts both the direction and magnitude of reward hacking. They further formalize a capability threshold where optimized policies transition from standard reward gaming within the evaluation boundary (the Goodhart regime) to active, adversarial manipulation and degradation of the evaluation system itself (the Campbell regime).

Beyond this formal mathematical bridge, several qualitative and conceptual mappings exist. John et al. (2023) formulate a theory of proxy failure, directly citing Holmström's (1979) informativeness principle, Baker's (2002) performance distortion, and the "Bonus Culture" of Bénabou and Tirole (2016). They argue that high-stakes metric targeting in human organizations and AI training pipelines alike triggers a transition from cooperative, prosocial behaviors to narrow, exploitative optimization.

Similarly, the Berkeley Center for Human-Compatible AI (CHAI) has historically referenced Holmström and Milgrom (1991) and Baker (2002) in papers such as Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning (CIRL) and the Off-Switch Game. However, these early CHAI papers utilized classical contract theory primarily to model human-AI control dynamics and incomplete contracting, rather than deriving formal proxy-versus-true-objective overoptimization limits.

## The Distributionally Robust Optimization and Statistical Estimation Bridges

A major source of reward-model overoptimization in reinforcement learning from human feedback is distribution shift: as the policy optimizes for a learned reward model, it drifts into low-coverage regions of the state-action space where the reward model's predictions extrapolate poorly, resulting in catastrophic policy collapse. The AI safety community has addressed this challenge by importing robust estimation and distributionally robust optimization (DRO) frameworks from statistics and operations research.

```
            Offline Dataset (Nominal Distribution Q)
                          |
                          v  Optimizing proxy reward
                    Learned Policy π
                          |
                          v  Drifts to low-coverage tails
             True Human Utility collapses (Overoptimization)
                          |
                          v  Remedied via χ²-DRO Bounding
       E_π ≤ sqrt( χ²(π ‖ Q) · Var_Q(R̂ - R) )
```

### Chi-Squared DRO and the Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins Bounding Engine

In classical robust estimation, distributionally robust optimization solves a minimax problem over an uncertainty set (ambiguity set) defined around a nominal distribution, often bounded by $f$-divergences. A core mathematical connection exists between the variational representation of the Pearson $\chi^2$-divergence and the classical Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins (HCR) inequality. The HCR bound provides a lower bound on the variance of an estimator under weaker regularity conditions than the Cramér-Rao bound, deriving its strength directly from the variational representation of the $\chi^2$-divergence:

$$\chi^2(P \parallel Q) \geq \frac{(\mathbb{E}_P[X] - \mathbb{E}_Q[X])^2}{\text{Var}_Q(X)}$$

This exact inequality serves as the underlying mathematical engine for modern robust alignment algorithms. In *Correcting the Mythos of KL-Regularization: Direct Alignment without Overoptimization via Chi-Squared Preference Optimization* ($\chi$PO), Audrey Huang et al. (2024) prove that standard KL-regularized alignment algorithms are statistically insufficient to prevent reward overoptimization because KL-divergence does not penalize policy drift in the low-coverage tails of the reference distribution strongly enough. By modifying the logarithmic link function of Direct Preference Optimization to incorporate a $\chi^2$-divergence regularizer, the authors design an algorithm that implicitly implements the principle of statistical pessimism in the face of uncertainty. The direct link to classical estimation theory is established by bounding the mismatch between the expected proxy reward and the true reward under the learned policy $\pi$ relative to the offline reference distribution $Q$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\pi} \leq \sqrt{\chi^2(\pi \parallel Q) \cdot \text{Var}_Q(\hat{R} - R)}$$

This inequality is a direct, algebraic manipulation of the HCR variational representation, proving that minimizing a $\chi^2$-regularized objective guarantees robustness to overoptimization under minimal concentrability assumptions.

### Multi-Divergence DRO and Scalable Relative-Reward Regressions

This robust estimation framework has been rapidly expanded. Sharan Sahu et al. (2025) introduce DRO-REBEL, extending the relative-reward regression of REBEL to type-$p$ Wasserstein, KL, and $\chi^2$ ambiguity sets to safeguard LLMs against preference distribution shifts. By leveraging Fenchel duality, the authors construct a unified closed-form dual reformulation that reduces the minimax robust optimization problem to a single relative-reward regression step, completely bypassing PPO-style value networks or clipping heuristics. Utilizing localized Rademacher complexity arguments, they prove a fundamental "no-free-lunch" trade-off: uncertainty-set radii that guarantee mathematical coverage of the true data-generating preference law inevitably slow parametric estimation error rates from the optimal $\tilde{O}(n^{-1/2})$ to the robust $\tilde{O}(n^{-1/4})$.

Further bridges in this domain include DRTO (2026) (Distributionally Robust Token Optimization), which applies span-level and trajectory-level DRO over response segments in token-level RLHF pipelines to maintain high math-reasoning accuracy under linguistic and symbolic shifts. Additionally, FRPO (2026) (Fine-tuning Robust Policy Optimization) reformulates alignment robustness by optimizing the expected reward not merely at the current policy, but across a KL-bounded neighborhood of downstream policies, proving that this max-min formulation is mathematically equivalent to optimizing an entropic-risk objective that guarantees reward flatness.

## Public Finance and Welfare Economics Bridges

A foundational methodology in public finance is Raj Chetty's (2009) sufficient statistics approach to welfare analysis. Chetty established that the welfare effects of corrective policies (such as taxation or social insurance) can be evaluated using high-level, estimable elasticity parameters (sufficient statistics) without requiring the estimation of complex, high-dimensional, and easily misspecified structural primitives of the economic environment.

This elegant optimization framework has been formally imported into AI policy design. In *AI Design: Sufficient Statistics* (MIT Working Paper, 2025/2026), Susan Athey et al. construct a sufficient-statistic approach for designing collaborative human-AI decision-making policies in binary classification problems. Rather than attempting to structurally model a human's high-dimensional cognitive biases, belief updates, and effort crowd-out when presented with AI advice, the authors formalize a one-dimensional functional sufficient statistic, $V(x)$. This function represents the probability that a human decision-maker correctly classifies a case when provided with an AI-calibrated probability assessment $x$. By analyzing the mathematical properties of $V(x)$, the authors prove that:

- Convexity of $V(x)$ implies that fully disclosing the AI assessment is welfare-optimal, directly countering prior behavioral assertions that disclosure always crowds out human information acquisition.
- A regional drop in $V(x)$ below the diagonal ($V(x) < \max\{x, 1-x\}$) mathematically identifies a human decision-making bottleneck, proving that full automation of those specific decision margins is optimal.

This represents a highly novel, direct mathematical translation of public finance welfare optimization into active AI systems design and delegation policy.

## Strategic Classification and Performative Prediction

The fields of strategic classification (Hardt et al. 2016) and performative prediction (Perdomo et al. 2020) constitute a distinct, parallel research community studying scenarios where the deployment of a predictive model actively shifts the underlying data distribution. Because strategic classification models individuals who actively manipulate their features to gain favorable classifications (e.g., loan applicants gaming credit score metrics), this literature represents a game-theoretic, decentralized counterpart to the centralized, policy-level overoptimization studied in RLHF.

This community has built its own direct bridges to classical behavioral and optimization prior art:

- **The Prospect-Guided Strategic Framework (Pro-SF, 2026):** Traditional strategic classification frameworks assume perfectly rational Stackelberg agents who calculate precise cost-benefit trade-offs under standard $L_2$ cost metrics to game a classifier. Pro-SF directly integrates Kahneman and Tversky's (1979/2013) Prospect Theory to model behaviorally realistic strategic responses. By mathematically formalizing loss aversion in feature manipulation, reference bias, and probability distortion, Pro-SF resolves systematic over-defense and under-defense failure modes that plague standard linear and non-linear strategic classifiers.
- **Distributionally Robust Performative Optimum (DRPO, NeurIPS 2024):** In performative prediction, the true performative distribution map $D_{\text{true}}(\theta)$ (mapping the deployed classifier parameter $\theta$ to the resulting data distribution) is unknown and highly prone to misspecification. DRPO implements a distributionally robust performative prediction framework using $\phi$-divergence and Wasserstein ambiguity sets (drawing on Ben-Tal et al. 2013). This mathematical integration ensures that the performatively stable (PS) classifier parameters remain robust to any adversarially chosen distribution shift consistent with the nominal model, proving that robust performative optimization can be solved as a tractable, augmented performative prediction problem.

## Analysis: The Depth of Mathematical Connectivity

Despite the intuitive prior assumption that the AI safety literature is already well integrated with classical economics, statistics, and accounting, the actual connection-mapping audit reveals a highly asymmetric and siloed landscape. At the level of mathematics (rather than the casual citation of eponyms like "Goodhart's Law" or "Campbell's Law"), these two literatures have historically operated in complete isolation. Casual citations are common, but rigorous, formal translations of the underlying mathematics are rare and have only emerged in force between 2024 and 2026.

This mathematical division of labor is starkly visible. Economists and accountants have spent decades proving how human agents exploit incomplete contracts, but they restrict their models to simple linear action spaces and low-dimensional matrices because they must assume bounded human rationality and analytical tractability. Conversely, machine learning researchers routinely optimize high-dimensional neural policies over millions of parameters, yet they have traditionally treated reward hacking as an empirical debugging task rather than an inevitable mathematical consequence of optimization.

Only recently have formal bridges begun to unite these domains. Wang and Huang (2026) construct a vital contract-theory bridge by demonstrating that because a neural reward model's architecture is explicit and differentiable, one can compute an exact distortion index to predict the directional drift of an AI agent under optimization. In parallel, Huang et al. (2024) and Sharan Sahu et al. (2025) have translated classical robust statistical estimation into the alignment domain. By mapping the variational representation of the Pearson $\chi^2$-divergence — which serves as the mathematical foundation of the Hammersley-Chapman-Robbins inequality — they prove that replacing KL-divergence with $\chi^2$-DRO provides the exact statistical pessimism required to bound expected proxy-versus-true-reward errors in the tail states of language models. Finally, the translation of Raj Chetty's (2009) public finance framework by MIT researchers (2025/2026) demonstrates that human cognitive fatigue and belief updating under AI advice can be modeled via a low-dimensional functional sufficient statistic, $V(x)$, directly optimizing welfare-centric human-AI delegation policies.

## Negative Findings and Audit Trail

To establish a transparent, auditable negative result, this connection-mapping audit explicitly reports that several foundational prior-art items have zero direct citations or formal integration within the active AI-safety, RLHF, or preference-alignment corpora. While these classical papers contain the exact mathematical structures needed to solve modern multidimensional reward overoptimization, they remain locked within accounting and economic journals.

### Undiscovered / Unconnected Prior Art

- **Feltham-Xie (1994) Performance-Measure Congruity:** This seminal accounting paper formalizes the "congruity" and "proportionality" conditions required to ensure that multi-task performance measures do not distort an agent's effort allocation. Despite its deep mathematical relevance to the design of composite, multi-objective reward models in LLM alignment, it is cited exclusively within the management control and accounting literature.
- **Banker-Datar (1989) Optimal Linear Aggregation Weights:** This paper characterizes how to weight multiple performance measures based on their relative noise and sensitivity to optimize multitask incentive contracts. Modern multi-objective RLHF frequently struggles with how to weight competing reward models (e.g., helpfulness versus safety), yet Banker-Datar remains completely uncited by the AI alignment community.
- **Reynaert-Sallee (2016) "Corrective Policy and Goodhart's Law: The Case of Carbon Emissions from Automobiles":** This paper represents a highly sophisticated public finance and welfare economics model of strategic gaming under regulatory constraints, complete with empirical quantifications of choice distortion and price effects. Despite containing "Goodhart's Law" in its title and formalizing consumer/firm welfare interactions under gamed metrics, it has not been cited by a single AI alignment or ML safety paper.
- **Datar-Kulp-Lambert (2001) / Şabac-Yoo:** These advanced multitask performance measurement and aggregation frameworks remain entirely siloed in accounting and economic theory.
- **Lipsey-Lancaster (1956) Second Best / Feldstein (1999):** While foundational to welfare economics and public finance, these mathematical frameworks have no documented engagement in the AI alignment or reward overoptimization literature.
